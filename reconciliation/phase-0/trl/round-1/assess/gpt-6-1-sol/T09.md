## T09 — High-voltage power tethers and site distribution on regolith

**Current TRL: 2–3 for the integrated NEA system. Target: TRL 6 before KDP-0B. AD²: 5, with a credible argument for 6 if autonomous tether handling remains unresolved.**

Spaceflight demonstrates kilovolt tether operation, and terrestrial equipment demonstrates much higher distribution power. Neither demonstrates the required combination: **1–3 kV, tens to hundreds of kilowatts, repeated autonomous reeling, abrasive charged regolith, and multi-year vacuum operation.** The architecture’s adoption is not evidence that this combination works.

### Assessment assumptions

The architecture does not specify cable length, service life, connector operations or allowable voltage drop. For this assessment I assume:

- **20–300 kW per robot circuit**, at **1–3 kV DC**.
- **100–1,000 m one-way cable length**, subject to revision after site layout and anchoring studies.
- An **insulated two-conductor circuit**; neither regolith nor the asteroid is an acceptable assumed return conductor.
- **3–5 years of service**, corresponding to approximately **2,400–11,000 powered operating hours** at the stated 9–25% duty cycle. Insulation also ages while unpowered.
- Vacuum, NEA thermal cycling at 0.9–1.3 AU, radiation, charged abrasive particles, and low-gravity cable dynamics.
- De-energized mating as the baseline. Hot mating is a separate, substantially harder qualification requirement.

These assumptions define a test envelope, not a settled design.

### Electrical and site-budget reconciliation

At nominal voltage, \(I=P/V\). Across the assumed envelope, circuit current is approximately **7–300 A**. Selecting 3 kV reduces current and conductor mass, but increases insulation, termination and clearance demands.

For illustration, a 1 km, two-conductor copper cable with **10 mm² per conductor** has approximately **3.4 Ω loop resistance at room temperature**:

- At 100 kW and 1 kV: nominal current 100 A; approximately **34 kW resistive loss**.
- At 100 kW and 3 kV: nominal current 33 A; approximately **3.8 kW resistive loss**.
- Copper alone weighs approximately **179 kg**, before insulation, protection, reel and connectors.

These are preliminary nominal-current calculations, not a solved constant-power load-flow model. They show why cable length cannot remain unspecified; operating-temperature resistance and converter stability must be included.

For **6–20 robots at 20–300 kW each**:

- Installed robot demand: **0.12–6 MW**.
- Mean demand using 9–25% duty cycle: approximately **0.011–1.5 MW**, before distribution losses.

The average is compatible with parts of the **2.5–8 MW** site envelope, but the upper instantaneous robot demand is not compatible with a 2.5 MW site. Processing, auxiliary systems, storage charging and any visiting-tug loads must receive explicit allocations. **Duty cycle is not a coincidence factor:** common operating windows or recovery from outages can synchronize robots.

The required design therefore includes load scheduling, local battery buffering, converter current limits and a coincident-load budget—not just a cable.

## 2. Current TRL and unmet exit criteria

### Integrated subsystem: **TRL 2–3**

- **Lower bound, TRL 2:** met by a documented application with identifiable benefits and constraints: high-voltage site distribution replacing large robot battery inventories.
- **Upper bound, TRL 3:** defensible only for critical electrical functions by transfer from physical tether heritage, supported by application-specific analysis. It is **not** a claim that dusty robot distribution has been demonstrated.

**For advancement from the conservative TRL 2 assessment to TRL 3:** demonstrate and document key application-specific predictions—especially wear-to-breakdown behavior, fault energy and reel/anchor dynamics.

**For advancement from TRL 3 to TRL 4:** demonstrate a representative cable–reel–termination–converter–protection breadboard in the laboratory, with performance agreeing with predictions and a documented relevant-environment definition. No such integrated result is established by the cited evidence.

Fixed, protected distribution and mobile robot tethers should have separate test records. A successful fixed cable does not qualify a moving cable.

### Critical failure mechanisms

- **Arcing:** insulation damage, contaminated terminations, connector gaps, outgassing and transient gas release. Vacuum does not make damaged high-voltage equipment intrinsically safe.
- **Multipaction:** assess converter ripple and RF fields in exposed vacuum gaps. Classical multipactor is not automatically the dominant failure mode of a substantially DC cable; it must not be conflated with all vacuum discharge.
- **Reeling:** bending fatigue, twisting, snagging, dust ingestion, loss of tension and disturbance of the robot or anchor in very low gravity.
- **Connectors:** sealing, dust exclusion, contact resistance, dielectric surface contamination and safe robotic verification before energization.
- **Fault isolation:** source-converter response, cable capacitance, DC-link energy and possible battery backfeed determine fault damage. Isolation must prevent one damaged tether from collapsing the site bus.

The final demonstration reaches TRL 6 **only if the combined evidence validates the required subsystem and its scaling**. A clean-space tether flight alone would leave regolith-contact performance unresolved; a ground reel test alone would leave low-gravity handling unresolved.

Service-life evidence must include failure statistics and justified acceleration models. Increasing voltage or abrasion rate arbitrarily is not a valid substitute for years of representative operation.

## 5. Risk and fallback

**Risk class: architecture-change.**

Failure would invalidate the specified tethered robot arrangement, but not necessarily all NEA production. Possible alternatives include:

- Elevated or shielded fixed distribution with short replaceable robot leads.
- Shorter work cells with relocatable power nodes.
- Larger onboard batteries and docking-based charging.
- Reduced robot concurrency, shorter cable runs and lower throughput.

Each alternative requires renewed mass, thermal, productivity and delivered-cost assessment. A lower voltage is not a free fallback: it increases current, conductor mass and losses.

If no alternative meets the production and economic gates, the consequence escalates to **project-ending for 0B**, with the pre-committed fallback **0A only, Earth supply**.

## 6. Where others might disagree

1. **TRL 2 versus 3:** electrical-tether heritage can support critical-function maturity, but it does not prove the mobile regolith-contact application. A strict integrated-system assessor should retain TRL 2 until application-specific experimental results exist.
2. **AD² 5 versus 6:** the electrical path is comparatively clear; autonomous handling in weak, irregular gravity may have several unresolved approaches.
3. **Flight demonstration necessity:** TRL 6 does not inherently require flight. Nevertheless, a wholly ground-based claim needs convincing validation of low-gravity reel, anchor and contact dynamics.
4. **Architecture-change versus project-ending:** the chosen classification assumes power delivery can be redesigned without destroying delivered-cost viability. That assumption must be tested, not presumed.
