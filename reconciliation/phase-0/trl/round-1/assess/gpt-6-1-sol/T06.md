## T06 — No-melt carbonyl refining with CO recycle

**Recommended current TRL: 2–3 for the integrated NEA production subsystem. Target: TRL 6 at KDP-0B; AD²: 6.**

Terrestrial carbonyl chemistry is established, and commercial nickel and iron carbonyl production is strong evidence that the chemistry is not speculative. It does **not** establish readiness of an autonomous, closed-loop asteroid refinery. The largest gaps are representative-feed reaction rates and selectivity, low-gravity solids handling, reagent recovery, and sustained containment of exceptionally hazardous gases.

**Keep carbonyl refining and H₂ reduction as separate technologies.** Their chemistry, feed requirements, recycle systems and failure modes differ. Neither eliminates the need to demonstrate gas–solid handling in the selected gravity environment.

### Assessment assumptions

- **Evidence cutoff:** March 2026. The supplied October 2026 decisions are treated as planning requirements, not demonstration evidence.
- **Output:** 600–2,500 t/yr of accepted Fe–Ni product if carbonyl refining supplies the entire allocation. If its selected share is \(f\), throughput and associated circulation requirements scale approximately with \(f\), but containment and autonomy requirements do not.
- **Operating time:** 8,000 productive hours/year, giving **75–313 kg/h** product.
- **Feed:** Beneficiated native or previously reduced Fe–Ni. Carbonylation is not assumed to extract metal directly from arbitrary silicates, oxides or sulfides.
- **Product:** Powder or deposited metal meeting a subsequently agreed construction-feedstock specification. Finished structural parts are outside this assessment.
- **Duration:** Not specified in the revised architecture. For qualification planning, assume a one-year production campaign and a 3–5-year maintainable module service life. These are proposed requirements, not inherited facts.
- **Environment:** NEA external vacuum, very low gravity unless deliberate rotation is selected, abrasive/charged dust, thermal cycling at 0.9–1.3 AU, and radiation. The reactor itself remains pressurized and temperature controlled.

## 3. Requirements that dominate the assessment

### Fe and Ni are not one interchangeable carbonyl process

Indicative terrestrial process windows—not project design specifications—are:

- **Ni(CO)₄:** Formation can occur at approximately atmospheric pressure and moderate temperatures, commonly around 50–60°C, on suitably reactive nickel feed. High-pressure variants also exist. A refractory or passivated Fe–Ni alloy cannot be assumed to react like activated nickel.
- **Fe(CO)₅:** Industrial formation generally involves substantially higher pressure and temperature, commonly of order **100–200 bar and 150–200°C**, with process-dependent feed activation and conditions.

A low-pressure nickel stage therefore does **not** demonstrate an iron-production route. Iron pressure-vessel mass, valves, feed locks and cyclic fatigue must appear explicitly in the plant BOM.

### CO circulation, inventory and make-up

Ideal stoichiometric CO carried through the carbonyl pathway is:

\[
\mathrm{Fe+5CO \rightleftharpoons Fe(CO)_5}
\qquad
\mathrm{Ni+4CO \rightleftharpoons Ni(CO)_4}
\]

This corresponds to approximately:

- **2.51 kg CO per kg Fe**
- **1.91 kg CO per kg Ni**

For 600–2,500 t/yr product, the stoichiometric CO passage is therefore approximately **1.15–6.27 kt/yr**, depending on composition. At 8,000 h/yr, that is **143–784 kg/h**.

This is **circulation, not net consumption**. Actual flow can be higher because of excess CO, incomplete conversion and recirculation.

As a sensitivity calculation, a net loss equivalent to **0.1–1% of that stoichiometric passage** would require approximately **1.1–63 t/yr imported CO**. These percentages are not demonstrated recovery performance. Purges, startup losses, leakage, maintenance and feed chemistry must be measured separately.

**CO inventory cannot yet be rated numerically.** It requires vessel volumes, pressures, temperatures, residence times, buffers and carbonyl holdup. Annual circulation is not an inventory estimate.

### Selectivity and sulfur

The feed programme must distinguish:

- Native alloy versus oxide-coated particles.
- Sulfide-hosted metal versus accessible metallic Fe–Ni.
- Fe/Ni partitioning between reaction stages.
- Surface-area changes, passivation and incomplete conversion.
- Sulfur entering product, residue, gas cleanup or reactor surfaces.

“Sulfur poisons carbonyl refining” is too broad. Sulfur effects depend on chemical form, concentration and process conditions; controlled sulfur additions or activation effects in some industrial practices must not be confused with tolerance of uncontrolled sulfide-bearing asteroid feed.

### No melt does not mean gravity independent

Reaction chemistry may not require gravity, but the complete system still must demonstrate:

- Feed transfer through pressure boundaries.
- Prevention of particle entrainment and filter blockage.
- Powder/deposit removal without gravity-fed discharge.
- Carbonyl condensation or collection, where selected.
- Stable reactor contacting without uncontrolled bed rearrangement.
- Recovery after plugs, leaks, shutdowns and thermal transients.

### Toxic-gas containment

An uncrewed plant removes routine crew exposure but not containment requirements. Ni(CO)₄, Fe(CO)₅ and CO require material compatibility, leakage detection, isolation and safe decontamination. Escaped material can contaminate equipment and product, exhaust reagent inventory, and compromise licensing.

“Vent to vacuum” is not an acceptable routine recycle or fault-management strategy.

## 6. Risk and fallback

**Primary classification: architecture-change.** Carbonyl refining is currently a contested route, not an indispensable physical principle of the whole architecture.

If it becomes the sole qualified production route at KDP-0B, failure is **project-ending for the selected 0B production option**. It does not end 0A; the precommitted fallback is **0A only, Earth supply**.

Fallback order:

1. Narrow output to native-metal concentrate or other accepted feedstock, **only if Phase 1 signs demand for it**.
2. Select a separately qualified H₂-reduction or rotating-smelting route.
3. Reduce the committed production allocation if demand and economics still close.
4. Otherwise, do not authorize 0B.

Adding rotation is an architecture change, not evidence that an untested carbonyl system has passed qualification.

## 7. Where assessors may disagree

1. **TRL 2 versus 3:** Industrial chemistry can support TRL 3 for transferred chemical functions; stricter assessors will require representative asteroid-feed proof first. Both positions are visible in the range.
2. **Relevance of gravity:** Forced-flow reactors may transfer well, but powder collection, filtration and pressure-lock feeding remain unproven. “Gas–solid” is not sufficient justification.
3. **TRL 6 demonstration scale:** A modular architecture might justify a small relevant-environment prototype. A throughput-dominant design may require a substantially larger demonstration.
4. **Sulfur tolerance:** Feed-specific results may overturn either optimistic or pessimistic generalizations.
5. **Product definition:** Demonstrating purified powder is not demonstrating ready-to-use structural material. Any required consolidation must receive its own rating.
6. **AD²:** The principal disagreement is between “established process needing adaptation” and “several unresolved subsystem approaches.” Until closed-loop low-gravity handling is demonstrated, I favor the latter.
