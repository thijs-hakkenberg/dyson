## T12 — Autonomous handling, docking and transfer of 150–300 t payloads

**Assessment: TRL 2–3 for the architecture-specific integrated capability; AD² 5 for standardized, sealed cargo.** Existing autonomous docking and robotic handling provide substantial heritage, but do not establish readiness for autonomous transfer of 150–300 t loads between an NEA production site, cargo tugs and an uncrewed SEL1 receiving facility.

**Recommended reference:** mechanically restrained, standardized containers or monolithic product packages, with cooperative navigation targets, known mass properties, soft capture, hard-mate restraints and a robotic recovery capability. Loose-material transfer requires a separate development branch.

The range is not an average: **TRL 2 is supported for the specified integrated application; TRL 3 is defensible for selected critical functions by analogy to physical demonstrations.** I have not been supplied a test-correlated, architecture-specific dynamics model that would establish TRL 3 for the complete T12 capability.

### Assessment assumptions and boundaries

T12 includes:

- Site storage-to-tug loading and tug-to-SEL1 unloading.
- Container restraint, release, capture, docking and verification of load paths.
- Internal plant handling, including surface-anchored machinery and free-flyer interfaces.
- Autonomous fault detection, abort, safe retreat and recovery without immediate human intervention.

It does **not** assess the metallurgy itself, tug propulsion life or the SEL1 station’s complete structural readiness.

The required payload is **150–300 t**, but docking dynamics depend on **total participating vehicle mass, inertia tensor, structural flexibility and receiving-node boundary conditions**, not payload mass alone. These remain unspecified. The legacy background’s “10+ cycles in 15 years” is also not automatically compatible with the revised **1.5–5-year round trips**; cycle life must be reconciled before qualification requirements are frozen.

### Implications for the contested metallurgy routes

- **No-melt route:** T12 remains **2–3 for sealed packages**, but **2 for loose product** unless containment and discharge are experimentally validated. Carbonyl or hydrogen processing chemistry does not itself provide cargo-handling evidence.
- **Rotating smelting route:** **2–3 with a stationary, cooled-product buffer**; direct handling of hot product across a rotating interface is **TRL 2 on the evidence available**.
- **Narrow native-metal route:** **2–3 for restrained product packages**. Experimentally gated rotating modules do not earn handling readiness until their actual transfer interface is tested.

These are **handling ratings**, not metallurgy ratings.

### Loads that must be defined before a higher rating

For a cargo package approaching a much more massive receiving structure:

| Relative speed | Kinetic energy, 150–300 t | Momentum |
|---|---:|---:|
| **0.01 m/s** | **7.5–15 J** | **1,500–3,000 kg·m/s** |
| **0.10 m/s** | **750–1,500 J** | **15,000–30,000 kg·m/s** |

With a nominal **0.1 m capture stroke**, these correspond to approximately **75–150 N** and **7.5–15 kN** average arresting force, respectively. Peak loads can be substantially higher. For two free vehicles, use the **reduced mass**, include tug mass, and couple translation to rotation and structural modes.

Other necessary corrections to the background:

- **Inertia cannot be scaled from mass alone.** Container dimensions, off-centre cargo, principal moments and slosh or settling behaviour must be specified.
- At **0.05–0.15 g**, a 150–300 t load imposes approximately **74–441 kN** of effective weight if the entire package is handled within the rotating plant. Smaller internal batches would reduce this requirement.
- The cited **0.01–0.1 m/s²** transit acceleration is not consistent with the reference electric-propulsion power and payload mass. Assuming 60–70% thruster efficiency, the stated power and specific impulse imply roughly **40–180 N thrust**, before vehicle dry mass is included.
- Increasing transfer duration from **24 to 48 hours does not halve fleet capacity** when round trips last 1.5–5 years. It adds roughly **0.05–0.18%** to cycle time, unless local berth congestion, thermal limits or another bottleneck dominates.

For loose material, add contained vacuum/microgravity experiments measuring escape fraction, residual inventory, discharge rate, electrostatic adhesion and recoverable jams. Do not credit sealed-container demonstrations to that branch.

**Gate implication:** the architecture cannot promise a small, inexpensive 0A pathfinder and then treat it as automatic evidence for 300 t handling. If adequate scaling evidence cannot be generated within 0A, D-02 requires delaying or declining KDP-0B.

## 5. Risk and fallback

**Primary risk class: architecture-change.** Failure to qualify 150–300 t transfers need not invalidate production if smaller packages or permanently integrated cargo/tug structures remain viable.

However, it becomes **project-ending for the approved 0B configuration** if the delivered-cost model depends on that handling envelope and no alternative meets demand, economics and licensing gates.

**Preferred fallback hierarchy:**

1. Reduce individual package mass and increase transfer count.
2. Use cooperative robotic berthing with autonomous safe-hold/recovery—not reliance on real-time Earth teleoperation.
3. Keep product sealed and use a stationary buffer between rotating production equipment and cargo handling.
4. Use tug-integrated cargo structures where practical, avoiding repeated large-container manipulation.
5. If alternatives fail the four discriminating gates: **0A only, Earth supply**.

Loose-product transfer also carries a direct licensing dependency: docking success is not proof that escaped material meets the proposed **≤1 t/year/site** ceiling.

## 6. Where others might disagree

- **TRL 3 versus TRL 2:** assessors may credit flight-demonstrated docking/manipulation as critical-function proof. A stricter assessment requires architecture-specific validated dynamics before granting integrated TRL 3. I favour the latter for gate decisions.
- **TRL 6 without full-mass flight:** validated subscale orbital tests and full-scale ground tests may satisfy NASA’s relevant-environment criterion. Whether they do depends on dynamic similarity, not a prescribed flight mass.
- **Risk classification:** smaller cargo may preserve the architecture, or may destroy its economics. That disagreement belongs in the delivered-cost gate rather than being hidden in a readiness average.
- **Rotating-plant scope:** transferring small, cooled packages through a stationary buffer is substantially easier than moving the entire 150–300 t load inside a rotating plant. The architecture must choose rather than combine those interpretations.
