### Evidence and Current TRL

The architecture requires two distinct waste-heat rejection systems: a 50–800 kWth system for the cargo tugs (operating in clean deep space) and a 1–9 MWth system for the processing plant (operating in a dusty, microgravity NEA environment). 

**1. Cargo Tug (50–800 kWth, clean vacuum):**
The International Space Station (ISS) External Active Thermal Control System (EATCS) utilizes pumped ammonia loops to reject approximately 70 kWth via deployable radiators at ~275 K. Scaling this to 800 kWth represents a 10× increase. Furthermore, to minimize radiator mass, high-power electric propulsion tugs typically require higher rejection temperatures (e.g., 350–500 K for Power Processing Units and thruster cooling), necessitating different coolants (such as water or organics like Dowtherm A) than the ISS ammonia system. While the basic principles of pumped fluid loops and heat pipes are TRL 9, autonomous leak isolation and scaling to 800 kWth at elevated temperatures are currently at **TRL 4–5**.

**2. Processing Plant (1–9 MWth, dusty microgravity):**
A 1–9 MWth rejection requirement is 15–130× larger than ISS capabilities. More critically, the plant radiators must operate in the immediate vicinity of active mining and beneficiation, exposing them to abrasive, charged regolith dust. Dust accumulation degrades radiator emissivity and can cause localized hot spots or mechanical jamming of deployment mechanisms. While passive and active dust mitigation technologies (e.g., electrodynamic dust shields) have been tested in laboratories (TRL 3–4) for solar panels and optical surfaces, they have not been integrated and validated on large-scale thermal radiators in a relevant environment. Consequently, the plant radiator system is at **TRL 3**.

**Current TRL Range:** 3–5. The lower bound (TRL 3) is met by analytical and experimental proof-of-concept for high-temperature coolants and preliminary dust mitigation. The unmet exit criterion for TRL 4 (for the plant) is component validation of dust-tolerant MW-class radiator segments in a laboratory environment.

### Target and Difficulty

**Target TRL:** 6 by KDP-0B (project-ending for Phase 0B, as the plant cannot operate without rejecting its waste heat).
**AD²:** 5 (New development; similar development has not been done at this scale and environment, but the path is clear through modular scaling and applied dust-shielding).

### Path to Target

1.  **Coolant & Materials Selection (Tug & Plant):** Analysis and subscale laboratory testing of high-temperature coolants (300–600 K) and compatible loop materials. (Reaches TRL 4).
2.  **Dust Mitigation Breadboard (Plant):** Vacuum-chamber testing of radiator surface coatings and active dust-shedding mechanisms (e.g., EDS) under simulated charged NEA dust bombardment. (Reaches TRL 4/5).
3.  **Subscale Modular Prototype (Tug & Plant):** Thermal-vacuum testing of a ~50–100 kWth modular radiator wing, demonstrating automated deployment, fluid loop stability, and leak isolation valves. (Reaches TRL 5).
4.  **Integrated System Prototype (Plant):** Thermal-vacuum testing of a multi-module array with integrated dust mitigation and simulated thermal loads. (Reaches TRL 6).

### Risk and Fallback

**Risk if fail:** Project-ending (for 0B). Without MW-class heat rejection, the processing plant will rapidly overheat, rendering in-space production impossible.
**Fallback:** Drastically reduce plant throughput to lower thermal loads, or revert to "0A only, Earth supply".

### Where others might disagree

Others might argue that because radiators are highly modular, flying a 70 kWth system on ISS means a 7 MWth system is just 100 copies of the same thing, implying a higher TRL. However, this ignores the non-linear complexities of manifolding, pumping massive fluid volumes, autonomous leak detection/isolation across a massive area, and the severe operational hazard of electrostatic dust coating the thermal surfaces.
