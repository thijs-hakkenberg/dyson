### 1. Evidence

The baseline for autonomous rendezvous and docking (AR&D) and cargo transfer is established by ISS operations, but the scale and autonomy required for Project Dyson far exceed current flight heritage. 
- **Automated Transfer Vehicle (ATV) / Progress / Cygnus / Dragon:** Demonstrate autonomous or ground-supervised rendezvous and docking/berthing. The ATV (ESA, 2008-2015) had a mass of ~20 t. 
- **Space Shuttle:** Docked ~100 t to the ISS, but this was a piloted, human-in-the-loop operation, not autonomous.
- **Canadarm2 (SSRMS):** Manipulates payloads up to ~116 t (Space Shuttle orbiter during pitch maneuver, though typically handles much smaller modules like the 15 t Columbus module) at extremely low velocities, but is human-operated.
- **Orbital Express (DARPA, 2007):** Demonstrated fully autonomous capture and fluid/hardware transfer, but at the ~1 t scale.

The required scale is 150–300 t of payload handling and transfer, fully autonomously, in deep space (SEL1/NEA). The scale ratio is roughly 10–15× for autonomous docking mass, and entirely unprecedented for autonomous robotic *transfer* of such massive discrete containers.

### 2. Current TRL

**TRL 3–4.** 
The basic principles of contact dynamics, GNC, and robotic manipulation are well understood and flight-proven at smaller scales (TRL 9). However, the specific application—autonomous capture, soft-docking, and physical transfer of a 150–300 t container with its associated massive rotational inertia and contact loads—has only been analytically modeled. 
*Unmet exit criterion for TRL 4:* Documented component/breadboard validation in a laboratory environment (e.g., air-bearing floor testing of scaled high-inertia capture mechanisms).

### 3. Target and Difficulty

- **Target TRL:** TRL 6 by KDP-0B. The ability to autonomously load/unload the tugs is critical to the 0B economic model and turnaround time.
- **AD²:** 5. New development; similar development has not been done at this mass and autonomy level, but the path is clear (scaling up existing NDS/berthing mechanisms and SSRMS-style robotics, combined with advanced computer vision).

### 4. Path

1. **Contact Dynamics Modeling:** High-fidelity simulation of 300 t container capture, evaluating capture cone tolerances and thruster plume impingement. (Simulation, $0.5M–$1M, 1 yr, reaches TRL 3).
2. **Subscale Air-Bearing Floor Testing:** 2D planar validation of the capture mechanism using heavy (e.g., 5–10 t) air-bearing sleds to validate the control algorithms and shock attenuation. (Experiment, $2M–$5M, 1.5 yrs, reaches TRL 4).
3. **Full-Scale Actuator/Latch Thermal-Vac Testing:** Laboratory validation of the hard-mate latches and robotic transfer joints under simulated NEA/SEL1 thermal cycling and vacuum. (Experiment, $5M–$10M, 2 yrs, reaches TRL 5).

### 5. Risk and Fallback

- **Risk if fail:** Architecture-change.
- **Fallback:** Abandon 150–300 t monolithic containers; divide payload into 20 t standard modules (ATV/Dragon scale) handled by conventional docking ports, which severely increases packaging mass fraction, transfer timeline, and operational complexity.

### 6. Where others might disagree

The assumption that scaling up docking mechanisms to 300 t is merely an engineering exercise (AD² 5). Critics might argue that the kinetic energy and structural loads of a 300 t mass in autonomous soft-capture require fundamentally new non-contact (magnetic/electrostatic) capture paradigms (AD² 7) to avoid catastrophic collision risks.
