This assessment evaluates the no-melt carbonyl (and H₂ reduction) metallurgy route for asteroidal Fe-Ni extraction, operating in microgravity with CO recycle.

### 1. Evidence
Carbonyl refining (the Mond process for nickel, and high-pressure processes for iron) is a mature terrestrial industry producing thousands of tonnes per year. However, its application to space resources relies on conceptual studies and limited terrestrial laboratory tests on meteorite simulants. 
- **Terrestrial Industry**: INCO and others have operated carbonyl refining at >10 kt/yr scales for over a century (1g, 1 atm to 300 atm depending on the metal).
- **Space Application Concepts**: Lewis et al. (1993) and subsequent ISRU design studies proposed carbonyl extraction for asteroid resources. 
- **Microgravity Gas-Solid Reactions**: There is no physical demonstration of high-pressure gas-solid carbonyl formation, continuous CO recycling, and subsequent thermal decomposition in a microgravity or relevant space environment. Microgravity multiphase flow and dust containment under high pressure remain unproven.

### 2. Current TRL
**TRL 2–3**. The basic principles are well understood terrestrially, and the concept for space application has been formulated (TRL 2). Some terrestrial laboratory experiments on meteorite simulants may satisfy analytical/experimental proof of concept for the chemistry (TRL 3), but the critical functions of gas-solid separation, high-pressure containment, and CO recycle in microgravity lack experimental validation. The exit criterion for TRL 4 (component/breadboard validation in a laboratory environment with documented definition of the relevant environment) is unmet for the microgravity fluid/dust handling aspects.

### 3. Target and Difficulty
**Target**: TRL 6 by KDP-0B (project-ending for this specific architectural branch).
**AD²**: 6. While the chemistry is known, designing a high-pressure, highly toxic (CO, metal carbonyls), continuous gas-solid reactor with near-perfect reagent recycling in microgravity involves several competing approaches (e.g., fluidized beds vs. mechanical agitation) and the path to a reliable zero-g system is not clear.

### 4. Path
1. **Terrestrial Simulant Extraction**: Bench-scale extraction of Fe and Ni from S-type/M-type simulants using CO/H₂, measuring kinetics and sulfur poisoning (TRL 3-4).
2. **Microgravity Gas-Solid Handling**: Parabolic flight or ISS glovebox experiments to demonstrate dust containment, gas flow, and separation in zero-g (TRL 4-5).
3. **Integrated Breadboard**: Thermal-vacuum chamber testing of a closed-loop carbonyl reactor with CO recycle (TRL 5).
4. **Subsystem Prototype**: End-to-end automated pilot plant in a relevant environment (TRL 6).

### 5. Risk and Fallback
**Risk**: Architecture-change. If the no-melt route fails to achieve closed-loop efficiency or microgravity reliability, the architecture must revert to the Gemini rotating smelting module.
**Fallback**: Adopt the rotating (0.05–0.15 g) high-temperature smelting architecture.

### 6. Where others might disagree
Proponents of the no-melt route might argue the TRL is higher (TRL 4) based on terrestrial chemical engineering heritage, assuming that mechanical containment of dust in pressure vessels is trivial in microgravity. However, D-02 strictly requires evidence in the relevant environment, and multiphase handling in zero-g is notoriously difficult.
