### 1. Evidence

The revised architecture requires storing 145–720 tonnes of propellant per tug and feeding it at 7–18 kg/h, alongside in-space refuelling capabilities. Two options are proposed: solid iodine or cryogenic/high-pressure argon.

*   **Iodine:** ThrustMe successfully demonstrated the NPT30-I2 iodine propulsion system in orbit (Beihangkongshi-1, 2020), proving the basic physics of solid iodine storage and sublimation feed in microgravity. However, this system stored approximately 1–2 kg of iodine and fed it at milligram-per-second rates. Scaling to 145+ tonnes and 7–18 kg/h requires managing massive thermal loads (iodine's enthalpy of sublimation) and preventing deposition in feed lines. Furthermore, refuelling solid iodine in space is entirely unprecedented; it would likely require transferring it as a heated liquid or gas and re-depositing it, which has never been demonstrated.
*   **Argon:** High-pressure argon storage is well understood but incurs severe mass penalties at the 100-tonne scale. Cryogenic argon (87 K) storage and transfer can leverage NASA's Cryogenic Fluid Management (CFM) portfolio (e.g., eCryo, Tipping Point demonstrations for LOX/LCH4/LH2), but argon-specific long-duration space storage at this scale lacks direct flight heritage.

### 2. Current TRL

**TRL 2–3**
While small-scale iodine sublimation is TRL 9, the required scale (100,000× larger) and the feed rates fundamentally change the thermal and fluid dynamics. In-space transfer of solid/sublimating iodine is TRL 2 (concept formulated). Cryogenic argon storage and transfer is TRL 3–4 by analogy to LOX/LCH4 CFM efforts, but lacks argon-specific breadboard validation for multi-year deep space missions. 

*Unmet exit criterion for TRL 4:* Component and/or breadboard validation in a laboratory environment of a 10+ kg/h iodine sublimation feed system, or a sub-scale cryogenic argon transfer system.

### 3. Target and Difficulty

*   **Target:** TRL 6 by KDP-0B.
*   **AD²:** 6. There are several competing approaches (Iodine vs. Argon, cryo vs. gas, solid transfer vs. liquid transfer), and the path to scaling iodine sublimation by 5 orders of magnitude while managing multi-kilowatt thermal phase-change loads in microgravity is not clear.

### 4. Path

1.  **High-Rate Sublimation Breadboard:** Laboratory experiment to demonstrate 10–20 kg/h iodine sublimation, measuring thermal power requirements, deposition risks, and valve reliability. (Scale: 1:1 feed rate, 1:1000 storage; $2M–$5M; 2–3 years; reaches TRL 4).
2.  **Propellant Transfer Formulation:** Analytical and experimental study on transferring iodine (liquid vs. gas phase) or cryogenic argon between vehicles in vacuum. ($1M–$3M; 1–2 years; reaches TRL 3).
3.  **Sub-scale Thermal-Vacuum Prototype:** Testing a 1-tonne scale storage and feed tank in a thermal-vacuum chamber to validate zero-g heat transfer models for the phase change. ($10M–$25M; 3–4 years; reaches TRL 5).

### 5. Risk and Fallback

*   **Risk:** Architecture-change. If high-density/cheap propellants (iodine/argon) cannot be stored and fed at this scale, the architecture must revert to Xenon or Krypton (which destroys the economic model due to Earth-supply costs) or drastically reduce tug size (increasing fleet size and operational complexity).
*   **Fallback:** Use high-pressure Krypton with smaller (10–20 t propellant) tugs, accepting the severe tankage mass fraction and economic penalties.

### 6. Where others might disagree

Proponents of iodine propulsion often treat the scaling of sublimation as a trivial thermal engineering problem (simply applying more heat). However, uniformly heating a 100-tonne block of solid iodine in microgravity, managing the expanding gas, and preventing cold-spot deposition in a 2.5 MW spacecraft's feed lines is a fundamental fluid-thermal challenge, not just a sizing exercise.
