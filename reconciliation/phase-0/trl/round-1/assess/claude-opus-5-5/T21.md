# TRL assessment: T21, Robotic maintenance, repair and spares logistics (with GPT's scope: site installation and recovery)

## 0. Scope and assumptions

**What is being rated.** Uncrewed, mostly autonomous servicing of a co-located NEA plant over 5–15 years. It has six critical functions:

| # | Function | Main stressor |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | Wear-part exchange on regolith-contacting machinery (bucket wheels and teeth, cutters, crusher and mill liners, screw and conveyor parts, seals, bearings) | Abrasive, charged dust in vacuum; galling and cold welding of fasteners |
| F2 | HV tether fault location, splice or replacement at 1–3 kV | Abrasion, arcing, regolith charging |
| F3 | Refractory and furnace maintenance (route-dependent; see §2b) | Heat, slag, rotating seals |
| F4 | Jam recovery and recovery of a disabled 2–3.5 t robot on a rubble surface | ~10⁻⁵ g; anchoring loss (rq-0-42) |
| F5 | Module installation and site set-up (GPT scope) | Large-mass handling on an uncooperative surface |
| F6 | Spares logistics: on-site inventory and resupply | Tug round trip of 1.5–5 yr |

**Assumptions.** Each one moves the rating or the cost if wrong.

- **A1. Light time and autonomy.** One-way light time is roughly 1–16 minutes. Routine interventions must therefore run autonomously, with ground supervision only.
- **A2. Gravity.** Surface gravity is about 10⁻⁶–10⁻⁴ g, which spans Bennu-class to km-scale NEAs.
- **A3. Intervention rate (low confidence).**
  - Operating hours: 8,760 h × 9–25% duty ≈ 800–2,200 h per robot per year.
  - Wear-part life is assumed at 500–5,000 operating hours. This is a terrestrial-analogy guess, not data.
  - The result is roughly 10–200 interventions per year fleet-wide, or about 10²–3×10³ over the plant life, before plant (non-robot) wear parts are counted.
  - This number is the key unknown. It drives spares mass and therefore the KDP-0B economics gate.
- **A4. KDP-0B date.** I assume KDP-0B falls about 6–10 years after KDP-0A, so roughly 2032–2036. No date is fixed in D-01 or D-02.

## 1. Evidence

### 1a. Space servicing heritage

These are clean, cooperative, designed-for-servicing systems.

**SSRMS (Canadarm2), ISS, 2001 onward.**
- Demonstrated large-mass handling and berthing of free-flyers (HTV, Dragon, Cygnus; order 10–16 t) using grapple fixtures, with crew or ground control.
- The arm's design payload capacity is about 116 t.
- Mass ratio to the 2–3.5 t requirement is below 1, so mass handling is not the gap.
- The environment differs fundamentally: free space, cooperative fixtures, no dust and no surface. The ratio is not applicable for F4 and F5.

**Dextre (SPDM), ISS, 2008 onward, and the Robotic Refueling Mission (RRM, NASA GSFC, from 2011).**
- Ground-supervised replacement of ISS ORUs and tool-based tasks on representative satellite interfaces.
- To my knowledge this amounts to dozens of operations over about 15 years. I am confident of the capability but not of the exact count.
- These were clean orbital tasks, without wear parts or regolith.
- Ratio in intervention count is about 10–100×, and the environment is not representative.

**Orbital Express (DARPA/Boeing, ASTRO and NextSat, 2007).**
- Autonomous ORU transfer (battery and flight computer) and propellant transfer, including autonomous capture, over about 3–4 months.
- Count ratio is about 10²–10³× fewer exchanges than required, and duration ratio about 20–50×.
- ORU masses were tens of kg. That figure is my estimate and I am unsure of it.

**ETS-VII (NASDA, 1997–99).** Autonomous rendezvous and docking with on-board manipulator experiments. These were short, clean experiments.

**MEV-1 and MEV-2 (Northrop Grumman, 2020 and 2021).**
- Docking to and life extension of client GEO satellites.
- This shows capture of a non-prepared client. It is not repair.

**OSAM-1 (formerly Restore-L).** Cancelled by NASA in 2024 without flying. This is evidence that the next servicing step, robotic servicing of an unprepared client, is still unflown.

### 1b. Surface operations, dust and failure heritage

These are mostly cautionary.

**Mars Exploration Rovers.**
- Opportunity was extracted from the Purgatory dune in 2005. The recovery took about 5 weeks of ground-planned commanding.
- Spirit became embedded at Troy in 2009 and was declared a stationary platform in 2010.
- These are the closest analogue to F4. They show that recovery from granular entrapment is slow, ground-intensive and not guaranteed, even at 0.38 g.

**Philae (ESA Rosetta, 2014).**
- The anchoring harpoons did not fire and the lander rebounded.
- This shows that anchoring in microgravity on a small body is unproven. It bears directly on F4 and F5.

**OSIRIS-REx TAGSAM (2020).**
- Bennu's near subsurface showed near-zero cohesion and loose packing. The spacecraft penetrated far more easily than predicted.
- Source: Walsh et al., 2022, *Science Advances*. I am confident of the finding and moderately confident of the exact reference.
- This bounds the void and boulder problem in rq-0-42.

**Apollo dust effects.**
- Seal, abrasion and thermal degradation of EVA equipment are documented in Gaier, NASA/TM-2005-213610, 2005.
- Apollo 16: the heat-flow experiment cable was broken by an astronaut tripping over it. This is direct evidence that surface cables are a failure point (F2).

**Galileo high-gain antenna (1991).**
- Partial deployment failure, commonly attributed to loss of lubricant and galling at restraint pins after transport.
- This is a canonical vacuum-tribology failure. It is relevant to fasteners and joints that must be undone after years of exposure (F1).

**Cold welding and fretting in vacuum.**
- ESA has studied this. I believe the reference is Merstallinger et al., ESA STM-279, around 2009, but I am not fully confident of the citation details.

### 1c. Terrestrial remote-maintenance analogues

These are teleoperated, at 1 g, with humans in the loop.

- **JET (UK) remote handling.**
  - The MASCOT teleoperated manipulator performed in-vessel tile and component exchange.
  - I am confident of the capability. I am unsure of the first-campaign year; I believe it was the late 1990s.
  - This is the best analogue for dirty, hazardous, long-campaign exchange. It is fully teleoperated, with no autonomy credit.
- **Hydro-Québec LineScout.**
  - A robot that inspects and makes minor repairs on energised HV transmission lines.
  - Confident it exists. Unsure of the in-service year (roughly 2006 onward).
  - This is the only analogue I know of for robotic HV conductor repair (F2). The voltages and geometry differ, and it operates in air.
- **Subsea work-class ROVs.** They exchange subsea modules routinely in the oil and gas industry. They are teleoperated, with an umbilical and a surface support vessel. I cite this as general industry practice, not a specific test.
- **Autonomous mining fleets (for example AutoHaul and autonomous haul-truck systems).**
  - Haulage is autonomous.
  - To my knowledge, maintenance and wear-part change remain crewed. This is a negative claim and I hold it with moderate confidence.

### 1d. Summary

No demonstration in any environment combines:
- autonomous or long-latency operation,
- exchange of wear parts that have been in contact with abrasive regolith,
- vacuum and microgravity or near-microgravity, and
- a campaign longer than months.

The strongest heritage, ISS and Orbital Express, has the wrong environment. The most environment-relevant heritage, JET and subsea, has the wrong autonomy level and gravity. Surface heritage (MER, Philae) is mostly failures.

## 2. Current TRL: 2–3 (integrated)

### 2a. By critical function

| Function | TRL | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| F1 Wear-part exchange, dusty vacuum, autonomous | 2 | Clean ORU exchange is TRL 7–9 in orbit, but there are no wear-rate data in vacuum with asteroid simulant and no dust-tolerant exchange interface test |
| F2 HV tether fault location and repair | 2 | TDR fault location is mature on Earth; LineScout is an in-air analogue; no vacuum or regolith insulation-life data (rq-0-60) |
| F3 Refractory and furnace maintenance | 1–2 | Route-dependent; see §2b |
| F4 Robot and jam recovery at ~10⁻⁵ g | 2 | Only MER and Philae experience, which is mostly negative |
| F5 Module installation on the surface | 2–3 | Orbital large-mass berthing heritage exists; nothing exists on an uncooperative granular surface |
| F6 Spares logistics with 1.5–5 yr resupply | 2–3 | Sparing models validated on ISS (crewed, frequent resupply) are the method basis; the inputs, failure and wear rates, are unknown |

### 2b. By metallurgy route

Each route is rated separately, as the architecture requires.

| Route | Distinctive maintenance burden | T21 TRL for that route |
|---|---|---|
| No-melt: magnetic beneficiation → carbonyl | Gas-tight seals, valves and pressure-vessel servicing. Ni(CO)₄ toxicity is irrelevant to humans here, but leaks are a loss and corrosion mechanism. Seal replacement must be done in a dusty environment. | 2 |
| No-melt: magnetic beneficiation → H₂ reduction | Moderate-temperature reactor (my assumption: roughly 700–1,000 °C). Seal and heat-exchanger servicing. Lower refractory burden. | 2 |
| Rotating 0.05–0.15 g smelting furnace (Gemini) | Refractory relining inside a rotating vessel, rotating seal and slip-ring service, slag-tap maintenance. There is terrestrial heritage for relining, but it is human or teleoperated at 1 g. I know of no autonomous or partial-g example. | 1–2 |
| Narrow native-metal route (GPT) | Dominated by comminution and magnetic separation wear (F1). Lowest thermal maintenance burden. | 2 |

### 2c. Exit criteria at each bound

- **Met at the lower bound (TRL 2).** A documented concept addressing feasibility and benefit exists in the Phase 0 architecture documents. Design studies qualify for TRL 2 only.
- **Unmet for TRL 3.** The criterion is documented analytical or experimental results validating predictions of key parameters. Three key parameters have no data:
  1. Wear life and intervention rate of regolith-contacting components in vacuum with representative asteroid simulant.
  2. Exchange-interface reliability after dust exposure and long dwell, given the galling and cold-weld risk.
  3. HV tether insulation wear and arc thresholds.
- **Why the upper bound is 3.** Some sub-functions are past TRL 3 by heritage: manipulation kinematics, autonomous capture, large-mass handling and sparing-analysis methods. The integrated rating is capped by the weakest critical function.

## 3. Target and difficulty

### 3a. Targets under D-02

**KDP-0B target: TRL 6** for F1, F2, F4 and F6.

My reasoning:
- 0B is a 5–15 yr uncrewed plant whose throughput depends on regolith-contacting machinery.
- Its delivered-cost gate cannot be credibly assessed without a demonstrated intervention rate and spares mass fraction.
- I therefore treat these functions as project-ending for 0B (see §5).

**Other functions:**
- F5 needs TRL 5 at KDP-0B and TRL 6 before Phase 0B deployment.
- F3 needs TRL 6 only if the rotating-smelter route is selected at KDP-0B. Otherwise it is deferred.

**Internal 0A milestones:**
- TRL 4 by the 0A mid-term review for F1 and F2 (lab breadboards plus wear and abrasion data).
- TRL 5 before the pathfinder design freeze, if a servicing experiment is to fly.

**Needed by:** KDP-0B, assumed about 2032–2036.

### 3b. Can TRL 6 be reached on the ground?

Only partially.
- Thermal-vacuum chambers with dust and simulant, plus gravity-offload or air-bearing testbeds, can give credible TRL 5–6 for F1 and F2.
- For F4 and F5, granular contact dynamics at 10⁻⁵ g cannot be reproduced on the ground beyond parabolic-flight and drop-tower durations of seconds.
- I recommend that a servicing and anchoring experiment flies on the 0A pathfinder. Without it, TRL 6 for F4 and F5 rests on DEM simulation validated only against OSIRIS-REx and Hayabusa2 contact data. That basis is thin and arguably does not meet "relevant environment, no waivers".

### 3c. Advancement Degree of Difficulty (AD²)

**Overall: 6** (several competing approaches; the path is not clear).

The competing approaches are:
- in-situ part-level repair,
- cartridge or module replace-not-repair, and
- whole-robot redundancy with disposal.

The choice depends on intervention-rate data that does not exist. Similar developments (ISS ORU exchange, JET remote handling) have been done, but not in this environment. That is why I did not rate it lower.

| Function | AD² | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | 5–6 | Path clear if cartridges are adopted; unclear if parts must be serviced at component level |
| F2 | 6–7 | Robotic HV splice in vacuum with dust is new; replacing whole segments is more tractable (about 5) |
| F3 (smelter route) | 7 | Autonomous refractory relining in a rotating vessel needs new engineering approaches |
| F4 | 6–7 | Anchoring in microgravity is unproven (Philae) |
| F5 | 5 | Builds on orbital large-mass handling |
| F6 | 4 | Method is known; the inputs are missing |

## 5. Risk and fallback

**Risk class: project-ending for 0B.**

The reasoning has two halves:
- The technology itself has a fallback, replace-not-repair, which on its own would make this an architecture change.
- But that fallback ships whole assemblies or whole robots by tug on a 1.5–5 yr round trip. That likely inflates the spares mass fraction enough to fail the D-02 delivered-cost gate. Failing the gate triggers the pre-committed outcome of "0A only, Earth supply".

**Fallback:**
- Design all regolith-contacting wear assemblies as standard swap cartridges.
- Overprovision the robot fleet (for example, an N+50–100% fleet), with disabled units abandoned in place rather than recovered.
- Pre-position spares sized by step 2.
- Accept throughput decay.
- If the resulting delivered cost fails the economics gate, take the 0A-only outcome.

## 6. Where others might disagree

1. **Heritage credit.** One could argue that ISS and Orbital Express ORU exchange is TRL 7–9 and that the environment difference only warrants dropping to TRL 4–5.

   My view is that the dust, wear, galling, autonomy, latency and duration differences each touch the critical function itself. They are not peripheral. Mass handling, which is the part heritage does cover, is not the hard part.

2. **Risk class.** A reasonable assessor would call this architecture-change. Replace-not-repair is always physically available.

   I classify it as project-ending because, under D-02, the economics gate is where the fallback probably dies. I am not certain of that; it depends on A3. If step 3 shows wear lives of 10⁴ h or more, the risk class drops to architecture-change or cost-increase.

3. **Ground versus flight for TRL 6.** Some will accept DEM plus parabolic flight as the relevant environment for F4 and F5. I do not, given that Philae and OSIRIS-REx both behaved off-prediction.

4. **Scope.** GPT bundles installation and recovery into T21. Claude and Gemini treat them as part of the robot and plant technologies.

   I kept them, but rated them separately, so the integrated number is not hostage to F5. If F4 and F5 are split out, T21 alone is still TRL 2–3.

5. **The intervention rate itself (A3) could be off by more than 10× in either direction.**
   - Low-speed excavation in near-zero-cohesion regolith may wear far less than terrestrial mining.
   - Vacuum galling may make fasteners unserviceable after long dwell.

   This is the single most contestable input, and the reason steps 2–3 come first.
