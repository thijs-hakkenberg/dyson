## 2. Where the models agree

- **No MW-class demonstration exists.** All three cite the ISS External ATCS (about 70 kWth total) as the largest relevant flight heritage. They give similar plant scale ratios: 14–130× (Claude), up to 130× (Gemini) and 14–129× (GPT). No model identifies a MW-class radiator that has flown or been ground-tested as a subsystem.
- **Modular scaling earns no extra credit.** All three explicitly reject "MW radiator = N × proven panels" as grounds for a higher TRL. They cite four untested behaviours:
  - manifolding;
  - deployment;
  - autonomous leak and micrometeoroid isolation over large areas;
  - common-mode dust exposure.
- **Dust is unqualified.** All three treat dust degradation of α/ε near an active regolith-processing plant as untested, and require dust and coating testing on the path.
- **Autonomous leak isolation is undemonstrated.** Claude and GPT both note that ISS ammonia-loop operations depended on crew/EVA servicing. Gemini lists autonomous leak isolation as an open gap.
- **Target and consequence.** All three set TRL 6 at KDP-0B and classify failure as project-ending for 0B. The ultimate fallback in each is "0A only, Earth supply" per D-02.
- **Rejection temperature drives area.** Claude and GPT both stress that the route-dependent rejection temperature matters more than the MW figure. Gemini also cites a 300–600 K range.
- **Rotating route.** Claude and GPT agree that rotation-dependent heat rejection is the lowest-TRL plant case, with AD² 6. Gemini does not address it.
- **Internal milestone.** Claude and GPT both set an internal milestone of TRL 4–5 within 0A.

## 3. Where the models diverge, and what would settle it

### D1. Tug scope and rating: 3–4 vs 4–5 vs 5–6

This is the largest numerical divergence. It stems mainly from how each model defines the tug heat load.

- **Claude:**
  - Argues the 800 kWth upper bound is meaningful only if it includes thruster losses.
  - Says those losses radiate passively from the thruster bodies at about 500–800 K and belong with propulsion.
  - Leaves the radiator loop (T1) with 50–150 kWth, which is 0.7–2× ISS.
  - On that basis rates T1 at 5–6.
  - Evidence relied on: ISS EATCS (E1), generic GEO heat-pipe bus heritage (E6, no specific citation), X3 at about 100 kW (E12) and Psyche SPT-140 (E13).
- **Gemini:**
  - Treats the full 50–800 kWth as a radiator-system load, which is about 10× ISS, at 350–500 K.
  - Rates it 4–5 on ISS heritage plus the change of coolant.
- **GPT:**
  - Says the PPU/thruster partition is unspecified and must be defined before rating.
  - Rates the integrated tug 3–4.
  - Declines to credit thruster self-radiation from the operation of smaller thrusters in space.
  - Notes the partition cuts both ways: a well-qualified self-radiating thruster could substantially reduce the radiator load; assigning all 800 kWth to a low-temperature PPU loop makes the tug much harder.

**What would settle it:**
1. A defined heat partition for the selected thruster and PPU. All three models flag this, and Claude's and GPT's path step 1 includes it.
2. Measured partition data from a tug propulsion/thermal test: Claude steps 7–8, or GPT's integrated tug demonstration.
3. The X3 thermal-data publication, which Claude says it has not cited.

A separate panel question is whether T03 should be split into a plant item and a tug item in the register (see D7).

### D2. Plant upper bound: TRL 3 vs 4

- **Gemini** rates the plant at TRL 3. Its reason is that dust mitigation has not been integrated on radiators.
- **Claude** rates 3–4.
- **GPT** also rates 3–4, but states that TRL 4 is conditional on obtaining applicable breadboard test reports. GPT says the architecture description does not establish it.

**What would settle it:** a documented lab breadboard of the route-specific rejection chain, with results agreeing with prediction. This means panels plus condensing heat exchanger, or a rotary joint for the rotating smelter. It also needs a documented definition of the NEA relevant environment.

Claude and GPT phrase the unmet criterion in essentially these terms. Gemini's phrasing is "component validation of dust-tolerant MW-class radiator segments in a laboratory environment".

### D3. Rotating-smelter bound: 2–4 vs 2–3

- **Claude** allows an upper bound of 4. It cites a high-temperature NaK radiator (E4, E5) and the ISS Thermal Radiator Rotary Joint (E3) as existing separately.
- **Claude also argues** that the rotating smelter may have the easier plant-level heat problem, because high-temperature rejection needs about 1/10–1/20 the area. On this view its rotary joint or spin structure is the harder part.
- **GPT** caps the rotating case at 3. It also cautions that "a hot furnace does not justify assuming that all plant auxiliary heat can be rejected at 600 K."
- **Gemini** is silent on this route.

**What would settle it:** either of the following.
- A rotary fluid-joint life test at 1–5 rpm and at least 10⁷ revolutions (Claude step 4).
- A located test record of an existing rotary thermal interface at the required coolant, temperature, environment and life (GPT).

Separately, a route heat-balance map would show what fraction of route heat can actually be rejected at high temperature.

### D8. Heat-load ceiling and radiator area

**The 9 MWth ceiling:**
- Claude states plant heat equals site electrical power (2.5–8 MW) plus any solar-thermal input. It concludes a solar-thermal smelter could exceed 9 MWth, and that the 9 MWth ceiling holds only for all-electric routes.
- GPT states the 9 MWth case "needs justification against the 2.5–8 MWe site supply."
- These positions are not fully reconciled in the submissions.

**Area figures** are on different bases and are not directly comparable:
- Claude gives two-sided panel area at ε 0.85 with a 200 K sink. Its 9 MWth figures are about 14,000 m² at 300 K and about 730 m² at 600 K.
- GPT gives total emitting area at ε 0.9, with no sink or sunlight. Its figures are 2,400–21,800 m² at 300 K and 150–1,360 m² at 600 K. GPT labels these "optimistic screening values".

**What would settle it:** the route energy balance step on everyone's path, done to a single stated convention.

**What would settle it:** the coolant and materials selection testing in the early path steps.

### 4c. Strongest evidence for the rating

The strongest cited evidence is the ISS EATCS. It is the only item all three cite, two of the three give it a source, and all treat it as confident.

Even so, the models differ on what it represents:
- design capacity: about 70 kW (Claude);
- published rated capacity: 35 kW per loop (GPT);
- demonstrated rejection: GPT says full-load calorimetry is not established by the sources cited.

The ISS EVA-repair record (Claude, GPT) is the main evidence used *against* crediting autonomous long life.

All other heritage either comes from a single model or is marked unsure by at least one model.

**GPT's qualification details**, offered as a checklist for steps 3–6:
- Dust: α/ε change against deposited mass.
- Leak isolation: demonstrated physical puncture, not command simulation only.
- Lifetime: endurance testing with a test-correlated degradation model.
- Gravity: relevant-gravity or flight testing where ground tests cannot bound two-phase or rotating behaviour.
- Scale: explicit validation of modular extrapolation.

**Fallbacks:**
- Claude: cap plant thermal power at what a demonstrated segment rejects within the mass budget, favouring ≥500 K rejection or direct hot-product radiation; then revert to 0A only.
- Gemini: drastically reduce throughput, or revert to 0A only.
- GPT: select independently qualified stationary processing and lower-power modular tugs if demand and economics close; otherwise 0A only.
